Why not every month?

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the plane of Earth's orbit, the ecliptic. It crosses that plane at only two points, the nodes.

A new or full moon usually lands a little above or below the plane, so the shadows miss. An eclipse needs that new or full moon to fall at a node.

Eclipse seasons

About twice a year, every 173 days, the line of nodes points at the Sun. That window lasts about five weeks, and every eclipse happens inside one.

So eclipses often come in pairs: a solar and a lunar eclipse about two weeks apart.

A cosmic coincidence

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, and about 400 times farther away. So in our sky they look the same size, and the Moon can cover the Sun exactly.

When the Moon is near its farthest point it looks slightly too small, and a ring of sunlight stays visible: an annular eclipse.
